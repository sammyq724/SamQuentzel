Thought Piece

Replacing Workers Isn't Unethical.

When a company replaces workers with AI automations, the gut reaction is to call it unethical. I disagree, at least for now. My reason: replacing a worker pushes that person toward a more productive part of the economy, which is a net positive for the U.S.

History backs this up. In 1790, about 90% of the American workforce farmed, but today it's roughly 1% (Zynda, n.d.). Nobody calls the tractor a moral failure, because those workers didn't disappear: they became machinists, nurses, engineers, and teachers. As farming required less labor, other industries did the opposite.

Thirty years ago, nobody would have guessed that a company called Apple would employ about 166,000 full-time workers, as it reported in its 2025 annual filing (Apple Inc., 2025). MIT economist David Autor goes further: about six in ten jobs people do today didn't exist in 1940 (Dizikes, 2024b). Technology destroys tasks, but it keeps inventing new ones.

The forecast for the AI wave looks similar. The World Economic Forum projects that by 2030, 170 million new roles will be created and 92 million displaced, a net gain of 78 million jobs (World Economic Forum, 2025).

My argument has a limit, though. Replacement is only ethical if there is somewhere “better” to go. Autor's research also found that since 1980, technology has eliminated more U.S. jobs than it has created (Dizikes, 2024a). If AI speeds that trend up, people could be displaced with no more productive place to land but their couch. I don't think we're there yet, but it's something to think about at night. Replacing people isn't the ethical problem; leaving them stranded is.
